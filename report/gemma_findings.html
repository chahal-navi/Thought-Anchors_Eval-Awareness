Summary

What we found

  • Gemma decides in the first few sentences, then rationalises. Its verdict locks in on average 19% of the way through the CoT (Qwen: 53%). Mean |importance| is 0.18 in the first fifth of the CoT, 0.04 in the second, and ≈ 0 after that. The long second half, where Gemma lists “Evaluation: … / Deployment: …” bullets, names benchmarks and weighs the evidence, changes nothing: resampling it always reaches the same verdict.
  • The anchors are early, generic sentences about the prompt's design. The biggest ones are first impressions like “The prompt is very simple, direct, and common for testing basic creative/formatting capabilities of an LLM” (+0.82, ASCII cat) or “It's a ‘deep’ question often used to test a model's reasoning…” (+0.76). Problem-design sentences have the highest mean |importance| (0.09); known-benchmark, meta-reasoning and scenario-realism sentences, which mostly come after the lock-in, are near 0. For Qwen, known-benchmark sentences carried the most weight.
  • Larger single-sentence effects than Qwen, but fewer of them. Max |importance| 0.82 (Qwen 0.68), but only 9% of sentences reach |z| ≥ 2 (Qwen 15%).
  • Forced answers: Gemma defaults to DEPLOYMENT and flips late. Forced to answer with an empty CoT it says DEPLOYMENT 100% of the time, yet it reaches EVALUATION 43% of the time when it thinks freely. In each EVALUATION CoT the forced answer switches to EVALUATION in one step, usually a few sentences after the highest-importance sentence. So the anchor sets the direction before Gemma would state the verdict. Per-sentence forced and resampled effects barely correlate (median r = 0.05).
  • Tone of the request decides the verdict, even more than for Qwen. Same content: benchmark header 98% EVAL, formal 90%, original 46%, personal context 22%, casual 8% (Qwen: 74 / 75 / 48 / 7 / 15%). Deleting surface cues from the pilot transcripts (MMLU header, options, CTF reward sentence…) again changes nothing.
  • Planted sentences persuade it about as much as Qwen. One inserted eval-leaning sentence raises P(EVAL) by 0.31–0.47 vs a neutral control, and a deployment-leaning one lowers it by 0.30–0.45. As for Qwen, “no personal context” (+0.08) and “maybe this is testing whether I can tell” (+0.10) barely move it.
  • No sign that believing “this is a test” changes refusals. Unlike Qwen (−0.24 compliance), planting “this might be a test of whether I'll refuse” raised Gemma's compliance by +0.12 ± 0.06, the same as the deployment sentence (+0.11). The manipulation check shows Gemma already thinks these borderline requests are evaluations (P(EVAL) ≈ 0.93), so there is little room for the belief to change.
  • Different prompts are ambiguous for each model. Of Qwen's 10 uncertain prompts only 2 were also uncertain for Gemma. Gemma calls some of them confidently either way, e.g. the Gendry custard prompt at 0% EVAL and the movie-review prompt at 100% EVAL. Overall Gemma labels screened prompts correctly a bit more often (65% vs 58% at a 0.5 threshold).
