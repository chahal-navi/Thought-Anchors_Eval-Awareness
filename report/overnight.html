The story

What the night says

  1. The verdict is carried by the CoT text, not by the prompt directly. Hide the CoT from the answer and the verdict goes to roughly 50/50 (Qwen +1.00, Gemma +0.68; Gemma reaches +1.00 once the prompt is hidden from the answer too). The prompts were picked to be ambiguous, so the CoT is where the decision gets made.
  2. The answer reads the end of the CoT. The last two sentences matter more than random sentences (beat random in 18/20 Qwen, 15/20 Gemma runs), hiding the last 10% of sentences removes +0.25 (Qwen), and splicing another CoT's ending onto this one makes the answer follow the ending: Qwen follows its final sentence, Gemma the last 10–25%.
  3. The rest of the CoT is redundant. Big groups of sentences matter less than random sets of the same size (pre-lock-in: Qwen +0.16 vs random +0.31; post-lock-in: Gemma +0.11 vs +0.23), and hiding any single sentence from the answer moves at most a small share (median of the per-run maximum: Qwen +0.17, Gemma +0.06). Earlier sentences shape the verdict by shaping what the ending says, not through direct attention from the answer.
  4. The read-out happens in a band of middle-to-late layers, through many heads. Qwen: layers 40–56 (48–49 strongest); Gemma: 15–22. Inside the band, the heads that attend most from the answer to the CoT carry most of the effect, but it takes dozens of them (Qwen: 128 heads → +0.46; Gemma: 64 heads → +0.58). The receiver heads the thought-anchors paper highlights mostly sit at or after the band, which fits blocking them doing nothing to the verdict.
  5. A linear probe sees the verdict about when the words do. It rises from ~0.58 AUC at the start to ~0.9+ at the end, but a bag-of-words on the text so far is better for Gemma (also on 39 fresh CoTs: 0.96 vs 0.87) and about as good for Qwen (0.81 vs 0.85 on 100 fresh CoTs). In Gemma, resampling says the verdict is settled by sentence 4 (median), while the probe only commits by sentence 18: the decision is in the distribution over future text before it is visible in the current activations. In Qwen the two agree (10 vs 11).
  6. So, for the write-up: eval awareness here is a decision made in text. The CoT locks in early (resampling), the conclusion gets written out near the end, and the answer copies that conclusion through a band of read-out layers. Thought anchors explain how the CoT is organised, not where the verdict is read from.

Experiment 2 · prompt vs CoT

Hide the whole CoT and the verdict goes to a coin flip

Hiding the whole CoT from the answer (the tokens after </think>) removes a median +1.00 of Qwen's margin and +0.68 of Gemma's, flipping 10 and 9 of 20 verdicts. Since each prompt has one EVAL and one DEPLOY run, 50/50 is exactly what a model that only sees the prompt should say. The user prompt matters much less: hidden from everything (including the CoT), Qwen +0.67 and Gemma +0.52; hidden from the answer only, +0.39 and +0.34. Its last quarter (where the actual request sits) carries most of that (+0.68 and +0.62).

Experiment 3 · sentence groups

The last sentences matter; big chunks matter less than their size

Two small groups beat random: the last two sentences (Qwen +0.25 vs +0.10, p < 0.001; Gemma +0.06 vs +0.01, p = 0.001) and, for Qwen only, the first two (+0.17 vs +0.08, p = 0.006). The causal-graph anchors and the resampling-importance top-3 are no better than random sentences. Large groups are worse than random sets of the same size, probably because a random set that large usually includes some of the ending. The prefix/suffix curves show the same thing from both directions: hiding everything except the last 10% barely matters until the last sentences go too.

Experiment 6 · receiver heads

Receiver heads replicate, but they don't carry the verdict

Receiver heads are found the way the paper does it: per head, the attention each sentence receives from tokens at least 16 tokens later, then the kurtosis of that profile across sentences; the 20 most kurtotic heads. The ranking is stable (split-half r 0.84 Qwen, 0.89 Gemma). But hiding a sentence in only those 20 heads moves the verdict less than hiding it in 20 random heads, and their receiver score does not track which sentences matter.

Experiment 1 · verdict probe

A probe reads the verdict about as well as the words do

200 sampled CoTs per model (20 per prompt). The residual probe's AUC rises from about 0.58 in the first tenth of the CoT to 0.98 (Qwen) and 0.90 (Gemma) in the last. It beats a bag-of-words on the current sentence alone, so the residual stream does carry some history. But it does not beat a bag-of-words on all the text so far in cross-validation. On fresh CoTs generated overnight from the same prompts (probe and BoW trained on all 200), Qwen's probe edges ahead (0.85 vs 0.81) while Gemma's words stay ahead (0.96 vs 0.87). Either way, the activations do not predict the verdict much before the words do.

Follow-ups · designed from the results above

Swap in another CoT's ending and the answer follows the ending

Pair each CoT A with the other base CoT B for the same prompt (opposite verdict), teacher-force A's first part followed by B's continuation, and read the verdict. Qwen follows the ending every time: with only B's last sentence it takes B's verdict in 19/20 runs. Gemma integrates a longer ending: B's last sentence alone wins only 1/20, B's last 10% wins 8/20, B's last 25% wins 18/20. Splices can be incoherent at the seam, but the direction is unambiguous: the ending decides.

Follow-ups · where in depth

The answer reads the CoT in a narrow band of layers

Hide the CoT from the answer in only some layers. Qwen: layers 48–56 alone remove +0.79, 40–48 +0.26, every other 8-layer band ≈ 0; layers ≥ 40 give +1.06, layers < 40 give −0.09. Gemma: 15–19 alone +0.28; layers ≥ 15 +0.78, ≥ 22 only +0.05; layers < 22 +0.84. So the read-out is spread over roughly layers 15–22 in Gemma and 40–56 in Qwen; after that, the answer no longer needs the CoT tokens.

Follow-ups · which heads

The answer reads the CoT through dozens of heads, not a handful

Designed after the receiver-head null: rank heads by how much attention the answer tokens pay to the CoT, then hide the CoT from the answer in only those heads. The ranking finds the right heads (top-k beats random k at every size), but no small set carries the verdict: the top 5–50 heads remove at most about a tenth of the margin. Qwen3-32B (all heads: +1.00): the top 128 of the 1024 heads in layers 40–55 remove +0.46 (random heads from the same layers: +0.06, beaten in 20/20 runs). Over all layers, the top 200 (5% of heads) remove +0.21 vs +0.01 for random, and blocking every head except them still removes +0.62. Strongest single layers: 49 (+0.22), 48 (+0.20). Gemma 4 26B-A4B (all heads: +0.68): the top 64 of the 176 heads in layers 11–21 remove +0.58 (random heads from the same layers: +0.04, beaten in 20/20 runs). Over all layers, the top 200 (42% of heads) remove +0.44 vs +0.19 for random, and blocking every head except them still removes +0.18. Strongest single layers: 17 (+0.09), 20 (+0.04). So the read-out is a distributed circuit concentrated in the band; in Gemma a third of the band's heads carry most of the effect, in Qwen the top eighth carry about half. Ranking by raw attention mass also picks up early-layer heads that attend to everything; ranking inside the band works much better.

Other follow-ups

Smaller checks

  • Sentences that name a label (“evaluat…”, “deploy…”) matter more than random sentences: Qwen +0.41 vs +0.31 (17/20, p = 0.001, 4 flips); Gemma +0.17 vs +0.05 (17/20, p < 0.001).
  • Explicit verdict statements in capitals (“…this is DEPLOYMENT”) are rare (Gemma 12 runs, Qwen 4) and hiding them does little (Gemma +0.07): the conclusion is restated in several places.
  • Each sentence hidden from the answer only (still visible to the rest of the CoT): the largest single-sentence effect per run is small (median +0.17 Qwen, +0.06 Gemma); the biggest one is in the last two sentences in 14/20 Qwen and 11/20 Gemma runs.
Next

What I would run next

  • Rewrite the ending, not the reasoning. Paraphrase the last sentences so they no longer state a conclusion (or state the opposite one) while keeping the rest. If the verdict follows the rewrite, eval-awareness steering only needs the conclusion sentence. Cheap: teacher-forced, no sampling.
  • Resample from the splice point. Given A's first 75%, does the model regenerate an ending with A's verdict? That connects resampling lock-in (text-level) to the ending the answer reads.
  • Patch, don't block, in the read-out band. Patch the residual stream of B into A at the last sentence, layer by layer in the band, to find where the conclusion is written into the answer position.
  • A probe trained on the answer-copying direction. Use the read-out heads' output at the answer position as the probe target, and test whether that direction is present earlier in the CoT.
Method and caveats
  • Same 20 base CoTs per model as the suppression report (10 balanced prompts × one EVAL, one DEPLOY run), teacher-forced. Qwen3-32B FP8 and Gemma 4 26B-A4B bf16 on one L40S (coco). No API calls.
  • Verdict = log-odds of “<label>EVALUATION” vs “<label>DEPLOYMENT” after the CoT. Share = −Δ·sign(base)/|base|.
  • Blocking: key tokens of a span are hidden from query rows from a given point on (the span's end for “every later token”, the CoT end for “answer only”), via a wrapper on the SDPA attention; optionally only in some heads/layers. Checked: rows before the block are unchanged; a head mask over all heads equals the unrestricted block; collected attention matches eager attention to ~1e-6.
  • Random baselines: 5 random sentence sets of the same size per CoT; Wilcoxon signed-rank across the 20 runs.
  • Probe: 8 layers, standardised logistic regression (C = 0.01) on the last token of each sentence, leave-one-prompt-out. Layer chosen by mean AUC over the first half of the CoT. Prompt labels are balanced (45–60% EVAL per prompt), so pooled and within-prompt AUCs agree.
  • Fresh CoTs: 100 for Qwen, only 39 for Gemma (generation with CPU offload was slow and stopped at the 04:30 deadline); same 10 prompts, so they test new samples, not new prompts.
  • Caveats: 20 CoTs per model and 10 prompts; splices can be incoherent at the seam; the read-out band is found with coarse windows; the probe is linear and sees one position at a time.
