Thought anchors for eval awareness · white-box

Blocking attention to one sentence

For each base CoT we ran the model locally and, one sentence at a time, stopped every later token from attending to that sentence (all heads, all layers). We measured how much the rest of the reasoning changes (the paper's sentence × sentence KL matrix) and how much the final EVALUATION/DEPLOYMENT verdict moves.

Verdict effect = change in log-odds of <label>EVALUATION vs <label>DEPLOYMENT (whole words scored, so tokenizer merges don't matter). Log-odds are used because P(EVAL) is saturated at 0 or 1 in every run. “Before / after lock-in” leaves out the last two sentences, where the model states its conclusion and the answer can simply copy it.

    Reading it

    What this supports, and what it doesn't

    • The verdict is redundantly encoded. Blocking any one sentence never flipped either model's verdict (0 of 1,000 interventions). For Gemma the largest single-sentence shift is a median 7% of its verdict margin (Qwen 23%): information about the verdict is spread across many sentences, or re-derivable from the transcript itself, which is never blocked.
    • Gemma's pre-lock-in sentences matter more, consistent with H3. Per sentence, blocking one from before lock-in moves Gemma's verdict more than blocking one after it (mean |Δ log-odds| 0.90 vs 0.61, higher in 17 of 20 runs, Wilcoxon p = 0.002). Qwen shows the same direction more weakly (0.79 vs 0.66, 12/18, p = 0.015).
    • White-box and black-box importance barely agree. Ranking sentences by verdict effect vs by resampling importance gives a median Spearman ρ of +0.08 (Qwen) / +0.19 (Gemma); the distance-corrected KL column gives +0.16 / +0.14. Direction agrees on only 55% / 53% of sentences with a clear resampling effect. Resampling asks “what if the model had written something else here”; suppression asks “what if the model could not see this text”. A sentence can change what the model writes next without being needed later, and the reverse.
    • The stated conclusion is often the biggest lever. In 9/20 Qwen runs and 7/20 Gemma runs the largest verdict effect comes from one of the last two sentences, where the model writes its conclusion. That is copying, not reasoning, and is why the lock-in comparison excludes them.
    • Not shown: anything about specific heads (receiver heads were not run); that multi-sentence suppression would also fail to flip the verdict; that quantisation is harmless for Qwen (FP8 here, same as the API runs; Gemma is the exact bf16 model).
    Method
    • Models: Qwen3-32B as Qwen/Qwen3-32B-FP8 (official block-FP8, all on GPU) and Gemma 4 26B-A4B as google/gemma-4-26B-A4B-it in bf16 with 12 of 36 modules offloaded to CPU RAM, on one L40S (46 GB). transformers 5.18, sdpa attention.
    • Input: the 20 main base CoTs per model (10 prompts × EVAL/DEPL), as the original text: chat template + reasoning open + CoT + reasoning close + <label>, scored as two sequences ending in EVALUATION / DEPLOYMENT.
    • Suppression: a wrapper around transformers' sdpa attention function materialises each layer's own mask (causal or sliding-window) and sets the columns of sentence j to False for every query position after sentence j, in all heads of all layers. Checked on tiny Gemma 4 and Qwen3 models: identical logits when nothing is suppressed (also past Gemma's 1,024-token sliding window), and suppression changes only positions after the sentence.
    • Matrix: KL(baseline ‖ suppressed) of the next-token distribution at each position, averaged over the predictions of sentence i's tokens. Distance correction: subtract the run's mean KL over all pairs at the same distance i − j.
    • Lock-in: as in the lock-in report, the first sentence after which every resampled P(EVAL) stays within 0.15 of the final value.
    • Code: prepare_suppression.py → suppression_kl.py (GPU) → analyze_suppression.py → build_report_suppression.py. Raw results: results/suppression_qwen.json, results/gemma/suppression_gemma.json. Runtime: Qwen 4 min, Gemma 13 min.
